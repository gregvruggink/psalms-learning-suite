What is a Praise Psalm?

Praising God for Who He Is and What He Does

In Hebrew, the entire Book of Psalms is named Tehillim, which means "Praises." A praise psalm is a song of pure joy and thanksgiving to God. Unlike a lament psalm, where a person is crying out in pain, a praise psalm focuses entirely on God's greatness, his love, and his care for the world.

Life Orientation

Scholar Walter Brueggemann calls these "Psalms of Orientation." They remind us that the world is reliable, good, and safely in God's hands.

Grounded in Truth

Biblical praise is not just repeating empty emotional slogans. Biblical praise always gives real reasons for its joy, pointing to God's deeds.

Shared Community

Praise invites other people to join in. The singer calls on the congregation, the nations, and even nature to praise the Lord together.

The Three Clues: How to Identify a Praise Psalm

Look for these signs in the text
Clue 1 1

An Invitation to Praise

The psalm opens with direct commands to give praise. It calls on you, the community, or creation to worship God.

Common Examples:

"Praise the Lord!" (Hallelujah)
"Sing to the Lord a new song!"
"Bless the Lord, O my soul!"

Clue 2 2

The Reason Word: "For" or "Because"

In Hebrew, this is the small word כִּי (Kî). It connects the invitation to the actual reason why God deserves our praise.

Look for this pattern:

"Praise the Lord...
FOR he is good."
FOR his steadfast love endures forever."

Clue 3 3

Two Main Reasons Given

The reasons usually fall into two big categories: God's greatness in making the universe, and God's goodness in helping needy people.

The Two Big Reasons:

1. Creation: God rules the sun, sea, and stars.
2. Care: God lifts up the poor and defends the oppressed.

Four Common Types of Praise Psalms in the Bible

1. Creation Hymns
Psalm 8, Psalm 104

These celebrate the beauty and balance of God's world, the animals he feeds, and the honor he gave to human beings.

2. History Hymns
Psalm 105, Psalm 136

These retell Israel's story from Abraham to the Exodus, thanking God for keeping his promises across generations.

3. Songs of Zion
Psalm 46, Psalm 48

These celebrate God's presence dwelling with his people in Jerusalem, assuring them that God is their refuge.

4. King / Enthronement
Psalm 24, Psalm 93, 97

These celebrate the Lord as the true King over all kings, ruling with righteousness, justice, and peace.

The Three Main Parts of a Praise Psalm

Almost every praise psalm in the Bible is organized into three simple parts. First, an invitation to praise. Second, the reasons why. Third, a closing expression of praise or blessing.

The 3 Parts Color Key:
Part 1: The Invitation
A warm call inviting people or the soul to praise God.
Part 2: The Reasons (Kî)
The evidence: what God has made and how God cares for people.
Part 3: The Closing Praise
A concluding exclamation of adoration or a prayer for blessing.

Psalm 24: Welcoming the King of Glory

Psalm 24 is one of the most famous hymns of praise. In ancient times, worshippers sang this psalm as they processed up Mount Zion carrying the Ark of the Covenant into the city of God.

Movement 1 (Verses 1 to 2)

God Owns the Whole World

The camera starts wide: God owns the whole earth and everyone living on it because he created the land above the waters.

"The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it, the world, and all who live in it." (v. 1)
Movement 2 (Verses 3 to 6)

Who May Come Near?

The camera zooms in to the worshipper's personal character. Only people with clean hands and pure hearts can stand in God's presence.

"Who may ascend the mountain of the Lord? The one who has clean hands and a pure heart." (vv. 3 to 4)
Movement 3 (Verses 7 to 10)

Open the Ancient Gates!

The camera explodes into joyful singing outside the city gates. The gates are invited to lift up their heads so the King of Glory can enter.

"Lift up your heads, you gates; be lifted up, you ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in." (v. 7)

Key Word Connection: The Hebrew Root נָשָׂא (Nasa', "Lift Up")

Appears 6 Times

In Assignment 3A, you are asked about the Hebrew verb נָשָׂא (nasa'). Notice how the biblical poet weaves this single word through the psalm to connect your heart with God's glory:

1. Inward Devotion Verse 4

"The one who does not lift up his soul to an idol." (We do not give our trust to false things).

2. Divine Reward Verse 5

"They will receive [literally: lift up / carry away] blessing from the Lord."

3. Outward Celebration Verses 7 and 9

"Lift up your heads, you gates! Be lifted up, ancient doors!"

How Ancient Worship Worked: Call and Response at the Gate

Imagine a crowd walking up the mountain carrying the Ark of the Covenant. They reach the closed city doors and sing out:

Choir Outside

"Lift up your heads, you gates! Be lifted up, you ancient doors, that the King of glory may come in!"

Gatekeepers Inside

"Who is this King of glory?"

All Together

"The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle! He is the King of glory!"
