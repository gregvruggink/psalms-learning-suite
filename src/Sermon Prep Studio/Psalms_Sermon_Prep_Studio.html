BIB523 The Book of Psalms

Psalms Sermon Prep Studio 6-Stage Homiletical Workbench

Case Study:
Homiletical Architecture for Hebrew Poetry

"The Sermon Should Do What the Psalm Does"

Preaching the Psalms is fundamentally different from preaching epistles or narratives. The Psalms are inspired poems of worship trading in sensory metaphor, emotional trajectories, and covenant memory. This six-stage workshop equips you to move from rigorous Hebrew exegesis to a dynamic sermon that takes the congregation on the psalm's living experiential journey.

Current Text Target: Psalm 13
"How Long, O LORD? Will You Forget Me Forever?"
Individual Lament • 6 Verses • Descent, Desperate Plea, Sudden Covenant Pivot
Stage 1 • Rules of Engagement

1. Identify the Genre & Map the Emotional Trajectory

Determining the genre reveals the poem's structural grammar, standard movements, and sets the emotional arc for your sermon.
Expected Structural Elements: Individual Lament Form Grammar
Critical Preaching Pitfall:

Rushing to the vow of praise prematurely! A sermon on a lament that skips past the agonizing complaints to offer quick positive clichés ruins the pastoral medicine of the psalm. Preach the silence before preaching the song.

Emotional Arc Visualizer Pacing Curve

The classic "U-Curve": begins in despair ("How long?"), reaches maximum vulnerability in the petition, pivots abruptly upon God's covenant loyalty (ḥeseḏ), and ascends to ecstatic joy.

Stage 2 • The Mechanics of Hebrew Verse

2. Map the Poetic Structure & Parallelism

Before parsing individual words, diagram strophes (stanzas) and analyze line parallelism to avoid the fatal "Two-Point Fallacy."
The Number One Homiletical Error in the Psalms: The "Two-Point Fallacy" Robert Alter • Kugel Principle

Hebrew parallelism is almost never static repetition. James Kugel famously demonstrated that biblical parallelism operates on the dynamic: "A, and what's more, B." Line B sharpens, intensifies, specifies, or climaxes Line A.

The Novice Mistake (2 Separate Points):
"The heavens declare the glory of God, and the sky above proclaims his handiwork." (Ps 19:1)
• Point 1: Look at the heavens.
• Point 2: Look at the sky.
Result: A disjointed, repetitive, boring sermon!
The Exegetical Preaching Dynamic ("A, what's more, B"):
Line A speaks of majesty; Line B specifies the personal craftsmanship ("handiwork").
• Single Unified Point: Creation is God's active cosmic cathedral.
• Preaching Strategy: Use Line B's tactile imagery ("handiwork") to intensify the wonder of Line A!
Type 1 • Intensifying (Synonymous)

Escalation / Focus

Line B takes the general concept of Line A and makes it vivid, specific, or climactic. Preach the single theme with heightened volume on B.

Ps 13:1 — "How long, O LORD? Will you forget me forever? / How long will you hide your face from me?" (Hiding face intensifies forgetting).
Type 2 • Contrastive (Antithetic)

The Great Divide

Line B sets up an uncompromising moral or covenant contrast to Line A. Essential for wisdom and royal preaching.

Ps 1:6 — "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, / but the way of the wicked will perish."
Type 3 • Strophic Markers

Stanza Boundaries

Look for refrains, Selah pauses, changes in pronoun (I → You → They), or inclusios (bookends) to define your sermon's major movements.

Ps 42 & 43 — 3 strophes each ending with the exact refrain: "Why are you cast down, O my soul?"
Stage 3 • Sensory Exegesis

3. Unpack the Imagery & Governing Metaphors

The Psalms trade in vivid, physical language. Don't flatten the poem into cold abstractions; make the congregation experience the image!
A. Complete the Five-Sense Sensory Audit:
Sight

Caves, shadows, lightning, boiling waters, green trees.

Sound

Roaring lions, crashing waves, groaning bones, festive shouts.

Touch / Physical

Parched throat, muddy clay, crushing rod, healing oil.

Spatial / Kinetic

Sinking into Sheol, fleeing to crags, ascending Zion hill.

Visceral Peril

Hunters' snares, poison under lips, melting wax heart.

Anchor Metaphors of the Psalter (Grounding Without Guesswork)

God as Rock / Fortress (Mā‘ôz / Ṣûr)

Rooted in the precipitous limestone cliffs of the Judean wilderness (e.g. Masada, En-gedi). A rock is not a gravel pebble; it is a sheer vertical stronghold where foot soldiers cannot pursue you.

The Shepherd (Rō‘eh)

In the arid hills of Judah, sheep die without an experienced shepherd who knows where hidden wadis hold water. A rod fights off wolves; a staff pulls sheep from crevices.

The Pit / Mire (Bôr / Ṭîṭ)

Ancient cisterns cut into limestone. Once you slip into the cold mud at the bottom, there is no foothold and no escape unless someone lowers a rope from above (Ps 40:2).

The Preacher's Rule: Do Not Kill the Metaphor!

If you immediately translate "God is my rock" into "God gives stability," you strip the poem of its emotional horsepower. First help your listeners standing on the edge of the cliff with the enemy army below; then let them feel the unshakeable granite beneath their feet.

Stage 4 • Redemptive-Historical Navigation

4. Trace Canonical Context & Christological Trajectory

Locate the psalm within the 5-Book architecture of the Psalter, then trace its highway to Christ and the resurrection (Luke 24:44).
A. Macro-Canonical Location: Where does it sit in the 5 Books? Book I (Psalms 1–41)
Book I (1–41) Davidic Combat & Agony
Book II (42–72) National Kingdom & Temple
Book III (73–89) Exile & Crown Shattered
Book IV (90–106) The LORD Reigns in Exile
Book V (107–150) Return & Hallelujah Chorus
1. Christ as the Pray-er / Singer of the Psalm

Jesus takes our human flesh and prays this psalm. When David cries "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Ps 22) or "Into your hands I commit my spirit" (Ps 31), Jesus is the ultimate Singer on the cross.

2. Christ as the Subject / Lord of the Psalm

The psalm prophetically describes the ascended King. "The LORD said to my Lord: Sit at my right hand" (Ps 110:1) or the Anointed Son of Psalm 2 whom all kings must kiss.

3. Christ as the True Israelite (The Righteous One)

Where Israel and humanity failed, Jesus is the only man of Psalm 1 who perfectly delighted in God's Torah day and night, and the only one with clean hands and a pure heart who can ascend God's holy hill (Ps 24).

4. The Church Singing In Christ

Because we are united to the risen Christ by faith, we now sing these ancient songs with resurrection confidence, knowing our tears are temporary and the Kingdom is secure.

The Moralizing Trap:

Never tell your congregation: "Be brave like David." David was an adulterer, murderer, and flawed king. The hero of the Psalter is Yahweh, the covenant-keeping God who redeems David from the pit and who sent the true Son of David to absorb the wrath our sins deserved!

Stage 5 • Distillation of Exegesis

5. Formulate the Homiletical Idea (The Big Idea Forge)

Distill your exegesis into one Exegetical Idea, then translate it into one memorable, biblical Homiletical Idea that captures both truth and emotional posture.
Step A • The Subject

What is the psalmist talking about?

Formulated as a comprehensive question or precise topic without the answer.

"How does a covenant believer endure when God seems silent and absent in prolonged suffering?"
Step B • The Complement

What does the psalmist declare about it?

The theological conclusion and resolution given by the inspired poet.

"...by boldly voicing honest agony, pleading for intervention, and anchoring trust in God's unfailing steadfast love (ḥeseḏ)."
The Two-Tier Synthesis (Exegetical → Homiletical) Robinson Homiletic Standard
1. Exegetical Idea (Historically Grounded):
"David moved from prolonged agony over God's apparent silence to ecstatic praise by anchoring his desperate soul in Yahweh's unfailing covenant loyalty (ḥeseḏ)."
2. Homiletical Idea (The Big Idea for Contemporary Preaching):
"When God feels distant, honest prayer turns our deepest agony into bold songs of unfailing love."

2. Memorable Single Sentence
3. Emotional Posture Matches Mood
4. Christ-Centered Gospel Anchor
Stage 6 • Homiletical Isomorphism

6. Structure the Sermon to Match the Text

The sermon outline must mirror the emotional and psychological movement of the poem, taking the congregation on the same journey!
The Dry Propositional Lecture (Flattens the Poetry):
Point 1: We all face problems in life.
Point 2: We should pray when bad things happen.
Point 3: God wants us to be happy.

Result: The congregation receives a lecture about despair instead of feeling the desperate thirst and drinking the living water.

The Living Experiential Journey (Does What the Text Does):
Movement 1: Entering the Darkness of God's Apparent Silence (Vs 1–2)
Movement 2: The Desperate Cry at the Edge of Death (Vs 3–4)
Movement 3: The Miracle of the Covenant Pivot: Remembering Ḥeseḏ (Vs 5)
Movement 4: Christ on the Cross & The Risen Song of the Redeemed (Vs 6)

Result: The congregation walks through agony and arrives at genuine doxology!
