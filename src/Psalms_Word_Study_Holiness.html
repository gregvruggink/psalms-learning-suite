Qōḏeš & Qāḏōš: Holiness

In the Book of Psalms, holiness denotes the absolute distinctness, purity, and majesty of God, who is set apart from everything common, profane (ḥōl), or corrupt. It marks God's very nature, His sacred dwelling place, His royal mountain, and the ethical integrity required of those who enter His presence.

Lexical Foundation

1. What Holiness Means: Four Lexical Pillars

Grounding our study directly in biblical Hebrew lexicography (BDB, HALOT, TDOT) and the text of the Psalms:

Pillar 1

Separation from the Common

In biblical Hebrew lexicography, qōḏeš defines that which is fundamentally set apart from the ordinary, common, or profane realm (ḥōl). It denotes God's unapproachable majesty and supreme otherness.

Psalm Evidence:

"Let them praise your great and awesome name! Holy is he!" — Psalm 99:3

Pillar 2

Spatial Consecration

Holiness in the Psalms is not an ethereal concept; it locates itself in concrete geography. God sanctifies physical spaces: the temple sanctuary (miqdāš) and Mount Zion (har qāḏšō).

Psalm Evidence:

"Great is the LORD and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, his holy mountain." — Psalm 48:1

Pillar 3

Ethical Threshold Requirement

Because God's sphere is pure, moral evil cannot dwell with Him. The entrance liturgies of the Psalms establish that entering God's holy presence demands clean hands, a pure heart, and social integrity.

Psalm Evidence:

"Who shall stand in his holy place? He who has clean hands and a pure heart..." — Psalm 24:3–4

Pillar 4

The Inviolable Divine Oath

When God affirms an unbreakable covenant promise, He swears "by my holiness" (bə-qāḏšī). Because holiness is God's very essence, swearing by His holiness guarantees that His word cannot fail.

Psalm Evidence:

"Once for all I have sworn by my holiness (bə-qāḏšī); I will not lie to David." — Psalm 89:35

2. How Holiness is Used in the Psalms: Five Core Contexts

The psalmists do not treat holiness as an abstract theory. They deploy terms from the root Q-D-Š in five precise theological and literary settings:

Context 1 of 5 • The Transcendent Holy Name

The Transcendent Royal Name (Psalm 99:3)

English Translation (ESV):

How Holiness Functions in this Text

Holiness is the supreme divine characteristic that evokes awe. The phrase šēm qāḏšō ("His holy name", e.g., Ps 103:1, 111:9) underscores that God's identity is utterly uncontaminated by human vanity. In royal enthronement psalms, God's holiness demands universal acclamation from all nations.

Liturgical Keystone • The Thrice-Holy King

3. The Three-Fold Acclamation: "Holy is He!" in Psalm 99

Psalm 99 stands as the liturgical crown of the enthronement collection (Psalms 93–99). The psalm is structured in three strophes, each concluding with the solemn refrains: kadosh hu ("Holy is he!") and culminating in ki-kadosh Yahweh Eloheynu ("For the LORD our God is holy!"). Step through the three movements:

Strophe 1 Ps 99:1–3
The Exalted King Over Nations
קָדוֹשׁ הוּא

"The LORD reigns; let the peoples tremble... Let them praise your great and awesome name! Holy is he!"

Strophe 2 Ps 99:4–5
The Lover of Justice & Equity
וְהִשְׁתַּחֲווּ לַהֲדֹם רַגְלָיו קָדוֹשׁ הוּא

"Exalt the LORD our God; worship at his footstool! Holy is he!"

Strophe 3 Ps 99:6–9
The Forgiving God at the Holy Hill
כִּי־קָדוֹשׁ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ

"Exalt the LORD our God, and worship at his holy mountain; for the LORD our God is holy!"

Hebrew Transliteration Standards Guide

This application supports two distinct transliteration modes to assist both academic exegesis students and general readers:

Academic SBL Standard

Full scientific 1-to-1 phonological fidelity to the Masoretic text. Preserves spirant consonants (ḇ, ḏ, ḵ, p̄, ṯ), macrons (ā, ē, ō), and gutturals (’, ‘, ḥ, š).

Example: qōḏeš • qāḏôš
Simplified (General-Purpose)

Intuitive English phonetic readability without special diacritics. Ideal for congregational slides, small groups, and general teaching.

Example: kodesh • kadosh
Stress Rule Distinction:

Notice that the noun qōḏeš is a segolate noun with penultimate stress (first syllable: KO-desh), whereas the adjective qāḏôš has ultimate stress (final syllable: ka-DOSH).
